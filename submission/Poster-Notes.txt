Poster title: Private messages can leak without being read

The finished poster uses a plain white 24 x 18 inch landscape page and three columns: the problem, the change, and the result. A nine-player diagram highlights the active connections in one measured Mafia-chat window. The highlighted IDs come directly from the experiment, not from an invented example.

The result column distinguishes three observations: 8 of 8 normal-visibility windows matched the expected recipient sets, 8 of 8 protected windows lacked the needed metadata, and 6 of 6 windows matched after visibility was deliberately re-enabled. The normal and recovery totals include idle controls. Unavailable metadata is not labeled as zero activity or a universal attack-prevention rate.

The middle column shows protected startup by default and the player resource caps. A small banner adds the final lifecycle change: the game restores prior settings on shutdown, and --restore handles interrupted cleanup. The memory-test note explains that the soft throttle was temporarily disabled inside the VM to exercise the hard cap separately.

One-minute explanation:

My game already sent Mafia chat only to Mafia players, but that did not hide everything. In a controlled test, a Town player could watch which server connection threads became active and infer the private recipients. I added separate Linux accounts and hidepid=2 so players could not read that metadata. I also limited player resources and tested CPU load, process creation, and memory exhaustion. The game kept running and finished a match. The final part was cleanup: the launcher now restores the settings that existed before the game, and it can recover after a forced kill. I learned that private messaging includes protecting activity, not just message contents, and that protection needs a reliable setup and cleanup path.
